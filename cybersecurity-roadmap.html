Cybersecurity roadmap: from beginner to a first job

Four stages, the main roles, and the entry-level certificates people usually start with. Use it as a map, not a rulebook: you do not have to finish a stage before touching the next one.

The four stages

Stage 1: Foundations

How networks work, how an operating system works (especially Linux), and one programming language. This is the base of every security job.

Stage 2: Core security

The three goals (confidentiality, integrity, availability), passwords and identity, cryptography basics, web security, social engineering and privacy.

Stage 3: Practice

Labs, capture-the-flag challenges, a small home lab, and short write-ups of what you did. Practise only on systems you own or have written permission to test.

Stage 4: Specialise

Pick one direction and go deeper (see the roles below).

The main roles

  • SOC analyst: watches alerts and logs, decides what is real, and escalates. One of the most common first jobs.
  • Penetration tester: attacks systems with permission to find weaknesses first, then writes a clear report. Usually comes after some years in IT or defence.
  • Incident responder: contains an attack, removes the attacker and restores systems.
  • GRC analyst (governance, risk and compliance): policies, risk assessments and audits. Good for people who like writing and organisation.
  • Cloud security engineer: secures cloud accounts, identities and storage. In high demand.
  • Threat intelligence analyst: studies who is attacking and how, and turns it into reports defenders can use.

Certificates people start with

  • CompTIA Security+: covers the fundamentals and is widely asked for in entry-level roles.
  • ISC2 Certified in Cybersecurity (CC): an entry-level certificate for people with no experience.
  • Cisco CCNA: networking, a strong base for security work.
  • eJPT: an entry-level, hands-on penetration testing certificate.
  • OSCP: a long, hands-on exam respected by penetration testing employers; not a first step.
  • CISSP: a senior certification; full certification needs about five years of paid security experience, so it is not a first certificate.

Certificates open doors, but proof of real work gets interviews: a home lab, write-ups, and small projects you can show.

Tips for the first job

  • Learn to explain a problem clearly in writing: a good report is half of the job.
  • Apply for junior and support roles too; many people enter security through IT.
  • Keep learning a little every day. Twenty minutes daily beats five hours once a month.
